OverviewA listing was never the hard part

Selling maize online sounds simple until you follow the maize. Someone has to find the farmer, record what they actually have, agree it with them, put it on a truck, check it again at a hub, and get it to a school or a hotel that ordered it. In Uganda those steps are usually held together by phone calls and personal relationships.

When I joined Farmsell in January 2024, the company had already learned this the hard way. Its 2022 marketplace attracted more than 1,000 listings and 200 registered sellers, and nearly 90% of those sellers turned out to be middlemen rather than farmers.9 Direct connections with farmers and institutional buyers followed in 2023, and the business was moving into aggregation, logistics and fulfilment.

My job was to translate that operating model into products people could use. I worked across the product lifecycle: understanding the business, defining requirements, mapping flows, designing interfaces and working with developers until the built product matched the intent.

02 Market AnalysisWhy this market needed more than a marketplace

Agriculture, forestry and fishing made up about 24.7% of Uganda’s GDP in FY 2023/24.1 The 2024 census shows how personal that is: 61% of households grow crops, while only 17% are engaged in commercial agriculture.2 There is a huge base of producers and a much smaller formal market. Maize and beans lead the crop list, which is why weights, grades and units became the backbone of the produce record.

What earlier digital markets taught us

The most useful study I found was Bergquist and McIntosh’s evaluation of Kudu, a digital marketplace rolled out across 110 sub-counties. Only 26% of farmers given access posted bids, and just 2% completed a trade. Traders turned out to be the main users.3 That matched what Farmsell saw in its own 2022 data. Open digital markets attract the people who already trade for a living.

A second paper changed how I thought about verification. Bold and colleagues worked with 600 maize farmers in western Uganda, combining post-harvest services with a secure buyer paying for quality. Bags with a visible defect fell from 86% to 4%, and farmers earned 11% more per bag. Extension training alone had little effect.4 Quality improved because a real buyer was waiting at the end.

Physical loss raises the stakes further. The APHLIS estimate for maize in Uganda cited by Shee and colleagues is a dry-weight loss of 17.2% to 23.8%.5 Weight can genuinely change between the farm and the hub, so the product has to expect differences rather than treat them as errors.

The device reality

Connectivity set a hard design constraint. Smartphones were 34% of the devices on Uganda’s networks in Q3 2025, and feature phones were 56%.6 One recent study found that 94% to 98% of smallholder households own a phone, but only 34% of its sample owned a smartphone, with women at a clear disadvantage.8

The decision this led toFarmers do not need their own app to take part. The aggregator registers them, records their produce and captures their consent on a smartphone, so the device requirement sits with one trained person who represents many farmers.

03 User ResearchThe people behind one sack of maize

Farmsell’s supply chain is a relay. Each person holds the produce, or the information about it, for only part of the journey. I mapped the roles and their handoffs before thinking about any one user.

Farmer

Produces and supplies the crops

Shares farm and harvest information, confirms recorded details and estimated payment, signs for their batch and receives payment.

Aggregator

Connects farmers to the supply chain

Registers farmers, plans production, records weight, provisional grade and photos, creates batches and loads, and tracks settlement.

Transporter

Moves produce to the hub

Responds to pickups, confirms identity, scans batch QR codes, signs the handoff and reports incidents.

Hub operator

Verifies and prepares produce

Receives loads, verifies weight and grade, records differences from the declaration, packs and allocates.

Distributor

Handles last-mile delivery

Collects allocated produce at the hub, delivers it to the right vendor and records completion or issues.

Vendor

The buyer, ordering for a business

Orders produce, follows the delivery, checks what arrives and accepts it or reports an issue.

Two distinctions shaped the whole product. The aggregator records a declaration and the hub operator verifies it. The transporter moves produce to the hub and the distributor moves it from the hub to vendors.

05 User FlowsMapping handovers between people

Individual role flows were straightforward. The difficult part was the handover between roles, so I started with a cross-role blueprint.

Role flows

Deep dive: the aggregator journey

I designed the aggregator experience first, because the aggregator creates the record every other role depends on. The full journey has five phases. Onboarding happens once; planning, collection and dispatch repeat every season.

Onboarding asks a lot: identity, business and operating zones, infrastructure, mobile money and a signed agreement. I split it into six short steps with a progress bar and Save and remind me later on every step, so a busy aggregator can finish across several sittings. Verification uses a WhatsApp code because WhatsApp is what aggregators already use every day.

SituationWhat the design does
Incorrect codeError message, another attempt allowed
Code not receivedResend after a 30 second wait
Wrong numberEdit the number and request a new code
Uncalibrated scaleWarning that only calibrated scales are accepted for batch weights
Forgot PINRecover through WhatsApp verification, then set a new PIN

Creating a batch

Batch creation is the most important moment in the product, because it turns a harvest into a record with evidence and the farmer’s consent. I kept it to four steps and designed the exits as carefully as the main path.

Handing over a load

The handoff is where responsibility changes hands, so nothing about it is optional. The aggregator confirms the transporter by QR code, records the condition of each batch, takes two load-out photos including the number plate, and both people sign on the same device.

One status journey

Every batch moves through the same five states. The track appears on the batch detail screen, so an aggregator can answer a farmer’s question about payment in one glance.

Deep dive: the hub operator journey

The hub is where the aggregator’s provisional figures meet a calibrated scale. The hub operator’s journey covers five phases, from registering the hub to handing an order to a distributor.

Receiving is not verifying

The most important decision in this role was separating two moments that are easy to blur. Receiving confirms that the load physically arrived, in what condition, and releases the transporter’s fare. Verification is a separate, independent check that produces the figures used for the farmer’s payment, hub fees and buyer prices. The receipt screen says so in plain words, and the verification screen opens with a warning: measure independently, do not copy the aggregator’s declared figures.

Weight differences without arguments

Produce loses weight between farm and hub, so a difference alone is not a problem. The reweigh screen calculates the difference as the operator types and compares it with a 5% tolerance. Inside the tolerance, no reason is needed. Above it, the operator must choose a discrepancy reason. In the prototype example, 845 kg against a declared 850 kg is a 0.6% difference, well within normal shrinkage.

Grading then splits the measured weight across Grade A, Grade B, processor quality and rejected produce, and the shares must reconcile with the measured total before the operator can continue.

Packages that remember where they came from

Packaging turns graded stock into crates, sacks or boxes with QR labels. Every package keeps its parent batch ID, so scanning a crate at the buyer’s stall shows the history back to the farmer. A reconciliation table makes sure every kilogram is either packaged, recorded as waste or kept as bulk.

Orders: approve, or decline with a reason

An order cannot quietly stall. The operator either approves it, which reserves stock, or declines it with one of three reasons the buyer will see. Approved orders move through allocation, distributor assignment, a package check and a two-person handover.

Order statusNext action shown
ApprovedAllocate stock
AllocatedPrepare dispatch
DeclinedReturn to other orders
DispatchedAwait delivery confirmation

Deep dive: the distributor journey

The distributor carries two kinds of responsibility at once: someone else’s produce and someone else’s money. I designed every screen to answer three questions for a driver standing at a hub gate or a market stall.

  1. What must I do next?
  2. What is stopping me from continuing?
  3. What goods or money am I responsible for?

Three statuses, kept apart

An early risk was a single “done” state hiding money that was still owed. A delivery can be complete while its cash is still with the driver, and a fee can be earned but not yet available. So delivery, cash return and payout each have their own status track and are never merged.

One journey, two ways to pay

Cash on delivery and mobile money differ in a single step. Rather than duplicating the delivery flow, the payment step changes and the rest stays the same. A mobile-money order never asks the driver to confirm cash, and payment status comes from the system instead of a screenshot.

Fixing the signature hand-off

In an early version, leaving the signature screen could lose progress or appear to finish the delivery. The fix: saving returns to the checklist with every completed check intact, shows “Signature recorded ✓” with an Edit option, and the delivery completes only when the driver taps Mark as delivered.

Returning cash to the hub

Exceptions with an owner

A “Report issue” button alone leaves the driver stranded. Every exception was designed with a next action, a responsible person and a visible status, and reporting never silently marks the delivery complete.

Choosing how to receive, then waiting for the hub

The old flow assumed pickup at review but showed delivery tracking afterwards. Now the vendor chooses delivery or collection before reviewing, so the window, address and charges are known before submitting. Submitting is clearly not approval: the hub confirms the lot or explains that it is no longer available, and nothing is charged until then.

Paying without paying twice

Mobile Money is requested only after approval. A pending payment tells the vendor not to send another, and a failed one keeps the order and the payment reference so a retry cannot create a duplicate. Cash orders simply show the confirmed amount due at handover.

Receiving, recovering and buying again

When goods arrive, the vendor checks the bags and grade, acknowledges receipt and confirms payment before the order completes. Each problem has its own route that keeps the order reference: report an issue and follow the ticket, or request a cancellation that the hub must confirm. The receipt ends with “Buy this produce again”, which reopens the product with today’s price.

Questions I asked of every path

  • How many steps does the important task take?
  • Is the next available action obvious?
  • Where is a confirmation or signature needed?
  • How does the user understand the current status?
  • How can they recover from an incomplete or failed action?
  • Which information should this role see?

06 WireframesSolving structure before style

Information architecture

Each role sees what it needs. The aggregator has the broadest work, so the deepest structure. The transporter and distributor work on the move, so their structures stay shallow.

Aggregator wireframes

Before any colour, I sketched each aggregator screen with its real labels, field names, sample values and button text. Low fidelity kept the focus on content and order: if a farmer’s estimate or a step name did not make sense in a sketch, no amount of polish would fix it later.

Hub operator wireframes

The hub screens are dense with numbers, so the early sketches focused on where calculated values appear and how each step makes its limits clear.

Distributor wireframes

Distributors read screens in short glances, so the sketches tested how little each screen could show and still answer the three questions.

Vendor wireframes

The vendor sketches tested money language most of all: whether “submitted”, “approved”, “pending” and “paid” could be told apart at a glance.

Transporter wireframes

The transporter flow had not been shared when I captured the prototype, so I sketched it from the role’s responsibilities and the handoffs on either side of it: the aggregator’s load sign-off and the hub’s receiving step. The sketches tested one idea above all, that custody should only move when there is evidence: a scan for every batch, photos of the load, and two signatures. These are proposals to validate with the team and with drivers.

10 Design SystemOne language across every role

Five role experiences create five opportunities for drift. Reusable foundations kept them consistent and gave developers a shared vocabulary.

Typeface and colours

Accessible colour pairs

Brand colours were checked against WCAG contrast before they were given jobs. Only Farmberry is strong enough for body text on white, so it carries actions; the lighter colours are kept for large type, icons and backgrounds.

One status language

Every role reads the same six statuses, each set by exactly one role. A difference at the hub is a branch, not a replacement, so the original declaration is never lost.

  6. Supported quality assurance through interface reviews and refinements.

OutcomesWhat the work contributed to

Farmsell’s published materials report 18,700 registered farmers, 270 market partners, 82% on-time deliveries and a 30% increase in farmer income.9 Those are company-reported figures and reflect the combined work of leadership, operations, field teams, developers and partners. I would not attribute them to design alone.

My contribution was the set of digital experiences that supported that operation: role-based tools, structured workflows, reusable patterns and a closer link between design intent and the built product.

ReflectionWhat this project taught me

ChallengeLesson
Multiple rolesUnderstand the complete ecosystem, then design around individual roles.
A user who never opens the appDesign the moments where the farmer is involved, even on someone else’s device.
Verification and accountabilityTrust comes from evidence at every handoff, not one approval.
Consistency across rolesShared foundations make a product scalable.
Bridging design and developmentEngineering knowledge connects user needs with technical realities.

If I started again, I would bring structured field sessions with aggregators and transporters in earlier, then use their results to challenge my assumptions sooner.

Farmsell changed how I think about my work. Good product design begins with understanding the problem, and on a project like this the problem sits on a farm, on a truck and at a hub scale long before it reaches a screen.
